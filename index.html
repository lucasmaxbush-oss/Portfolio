Sheet 01 Senior capstone · PKSC 4200 · Spring 2025

Two packages for one fragile mug

Role: structural design & technical drawing · Sponsor: national industrial packaging company

The brief was a fiber-based repack for custom-printed ceramic mugs shipped straight to consumers. Two mug sizes, one package family. Curbside recyclable, under a pound for the 11 oz mug, and fast enough to assemble at a fulfillment station. The incumbent solution leaned on plastic film and loose void fill — it protects the mug, but it ends up in the trash and reads as cheap on the doorstep.

The sponsor asked for two answers. A production shipper that could run at 10,000 units a month on a die-cut, glued B-flute blank — and a premium "Cadillac" design with no cost ceiling, judged on the unboxing experience, but still required to pass ISTA 3A.

We audited what was already on the market, scored twenty-odd concepts against weighted criteria, and built both. Before any of that, though, we measured the product: the mug's own compression data showed roughly 4,500 lb of capacity standing upright and almost none lying on its side — so every structure that followed had one job, which was to keep the mug vertical and keep load off the handle.

The “Cadillac” Telescope box · coated E flute · passed ISTA 3A

The premium brief had one hard constraint and no others: make the unboxing memorable, and still pass ISTA 3A. The answer was a telescope-style box in three parts — a three-sided roll-over tray with a central cutout that seats the mug and shows its graphics the moment the lid comes off, a three-panel insert that puts printed graphics behind the mug, and a top shroud carrying the exterior artwork.

All three are cut from mottled white coated E-flute, chosen for print quality rather than strength — which meant the primary package was never going to survive distribution alone. So it ships inside a secondary cherry-lock box in C flute, with two pieces of food-waste-based foam: one cushioning the base, one glued inside the top shroud directly above the mug. That system passed ISTA 3A for both the 11 oz and 15 oz mug.

Testing Drop, compression, and the survival challenge

Two kinds of testing ran in parallel. In the lab: compression and drop testing of the mugs themselves and the packages around them, at the orientations the test plan calls out, recording both failure height and critical height. The handle is the governing failure mode — it goes first, at a lower height than any other feature.

And then the course's survival challenge: the finished package goes off a balcony onto paving, in front of everyone, once.

Sheet 02 Milwaukee Tool · Packaging co-op · Jun – Dec 2025

Foam inserts that fly marketing samples without damage

Role: insert design, prototyping, and ISTA test reporting

Marketing samples travel constantly — to shoots, to trade shows, to distributors — and they travel by air, which means low pressure, rough handling, and no second chance if a tool arrives scuffed. My job was to design custom inserts that fit inside the existing modular tool cases, so the sample kit protects itself and packs down into hardware the company already sells.

Each insert is die-cut foam, milled to the silhouette of the specific tool or accessory set it carries. The large-bit kit needed two layers: an upper tray of small accessories over a lower layer of hole saws and arbors, so the case reads as an organized kit the moment it opens rather than a pile of loose steel.

I ran ISTA testing across a range of products alongside the design work, then wrote and presented the reports — which is where the design feedback actually came from.

Sheet 03 Clemson printing lab · Corrugated fixtures

Fixtures for a working print lab

Role: design, dielines, and iteration on the cutting table

Two problems, both the unglamorous kind that make a shared shop work better. The cleaning cart had solvent bottles rolling loose on a steel shelf; the press area had sponges drying on whatever surface was free. Both got a corrugated fixture designed around the actual objects and the actual cart.

These are the projects where the iteration is the interesting part. The sponge hanger went through enough revisions to reach version 2.2, and the progression photos show the cart insert narrowing in on bottle diameter, wall height, and how much material you can remove before the shelf sags. Cut it, put it in service, watch what fails, cut it again.

Sheet 04 Corrugated structures · Clemson

A cantilever chair made of paper

Role: structural design, nesting, and load testing

A chair is a good way to find out whether you actually understand board. There is no forgiving load case: a person sits down, the seat cantilevers off the back, and every bending moment has to travel through folded paper to the floor.

The structure is a stack of profiled ribs cut from a single board grade — no fasteners, no adhesive, no frame hiding inside. The ribs interlock and the repeated cutouts do double duty: they take out mass and weaken the panel in exactly the places where it needs to fold, while the solid ligaments between them carry the load path down to the base. Flute direction runs with the load, which is the whole trick.

Then you sit in it. It holds.
